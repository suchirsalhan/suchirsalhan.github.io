Cambridge Language Sciences Symposium 2025: Ambitions for Language Science in 2050

A day of paired dialogues, poster slams, and networking across the language sciences at Cambridge, with a deliberately long horizon.

The Cambridge Language Sciences Annual Symposium is a yearly gathering of language scientists from across the University's disciplines, and the 2025 edition took the theme "Ambitions for Language Science in 2050".1Event site. It ran on Thursday 27 November 2025 from midday to half past seven at Cripps Court, Magdalene College. The choice of a 2050 horizon is a useful device: it pushes speakers past the next grant cycle and asks what the field should be trying to understand a generation from now, which tends to surface more honest answers than a survey of current results would.

Format

The programme was built around two research dialogues, each pairing two speakers on a shared question, together with two poster slam sessions, a poster exhibition over refreshments, and a closing drinks reception. Pairing speakers rather than running solo keynotes is a good way to keep the conversation genuinely cross-disciplinary, since the two people on stage rarely start from the same assumptions.

The dialogues

The first dialogue, on aphasia and AI, paired Professor Matt Lambon-Ralph of Cambridge with Dr Fatemeh Geranmayeh of Imperial College London, chaired by Dr Holly Robson of UCL. The second, on meaning and machines, paired Professor Tim Rogers of the University of Wisconsin-Madison with Dr Guy Emerson of Cambridge, chaired by Dr Matt Davis of Cambridge. Between them the two sessions spanned clinical language science and the semantics of computational models, which is roughly the range the symposium exists to hold together.

The poster sessions

I helped organise the poster sessions for the symposium, working with Sammy Weiss-Cowie and Shanshan Hu. The posters were presented in two slam sessions and then exhibited during refreshments, with the accepted list circulated ahead of the day. Organising this side of an event is mostly quiet work: running the call, reading submissions, arranging the slams so early-career researchers get a real audience, and making sure the exhibition has enough time and space to matter rather than being squeezed between talks. For a symposium whose whole point is bringing distant parts of the language sciences into the same room, the poster floor is often where the actual mixing happens, and it was satisfying to see conversations start there.

The event was supported by Cambridge University Press & Assessment. What I took from the day was less any single prediction about 2050 than the reminder that the language sciences at Cambridge are unusually broad, spanning clinicians, linguists, and machine-learning researchers, and that they benefit from a fixed point in the calendar where those groups are obliged to talk to each other.
