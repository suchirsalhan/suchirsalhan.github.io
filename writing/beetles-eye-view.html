A Beetle's-Eye View of Open AI: Building Slowly in a Field That Never Stops Moving and How Good Science Takes a Village

How a question about bilingual language learning grew into 330 open models, an interdisciplinary research community, and one of the most rewarding projects I've worked on.

The accidental making of 330 little language models

I didn't set out to train 330 language models; in fact, I think that would be a rather odd thing for someone to even want to do in the first place! But somehow I spent six months of my life dedicated to this quest and I learnt a thing or two along the way.

When I first started thinking about Beetle, I had a fairly modest question: does it matter how a language model learns a second language? However, this followed with almost 6 months, of course interspersed with working on lots of other cool projects, of experiments, infrastructure building, conversations with researchers across different disciplines.

But this has been the hardest project I have ever worked on. On more than a few occasions I wondered whether I'd bitten off considerably more than I could chew.

There were plenty of hard day's nights, usually involving Cloudflare GPUs, out-of-memory errors and the increasingly philosophical question of whether a training run would ever finish.

With the benefit of time, it is also what makes the project so rewarding.

Beetle has taken me through several research communities that I might not otherwise have encountered in quite the same way. It began with my interest in small language models and the BabyLM community, grew through collaborations with people working in open-source AI and computational psycholinguistics, and eventually found connections with researchers and practitioners interested in bilingualism and language assessment. Along the way, it became a much bigger project than I had originally imagined!

I wanted to write a little about that journey: not just what we built (which I've already documented extensively; see the Beetle website and my post on Hugging Face). I wanted to take a moment to focus on more human aspects of research.

It started with BabyLM

A lot of my thinking about Beetle grew out of the BabyLM Challenge and the community around it. BabyLM is how I came into the AI field. I've always found the central question behind BabyLM compelling: how much can we learn about language acquisition if we stop assuming that models need enormous quantities of data to be interesting?

The challenge brings together people interested in language learning, cognitive science, linguistics and machine learning. It was an unusually stimulating environment in which to think about models not simply as systems to scale, but as systems whose learning experiences we could study.

I started wondering whether we could take this a little further; most people are multilingual, but for years the BabyLM community was focussed on English. That was until a wonderful effort led by Jaap Jumelet, where we released BabyBabelLM. Rather than just controlling how much language a model encountered, this now allowed me to ask what happened to language models if we controlled how it encountered different languages?

In my PhD work with my supervisor Paula Buttery, we were thinking about building computational models of second language acquisition. Many of our early conversations were rooted in computational linguistics and modelling of acquisition. My first year, however, introduced me to new techniques in learning dynamics, interpretability and tokenization. I was also forced to think about more practical questions guided by the "north star" of priorities of the team of AI researchers and managers at Cambridge University Press & Assessment. Their interest in practical language assessment introduced another dimension to the project. It was a reminder that questions about language proficiency aren't confined to academic benchmarks. They matter to educators, learners and the people responsible for assessing language ability in the real world.

The people who made Beetle what it became

One of my favourite things about working on Beetle was how many different research communities it brought together! I think this has really shaped the flavour of research that I value, where we can speak to multiple communities through shared infrastructure.

The BabyLM community gave me an initial home for thinking about sample-efficient language learning. But my involvement with the open-source AI community, particularly EleutherAI, also had a significant influence on how I approached the project. I'd invited Catherine Arnett and Tyler Chang to give a seminar in Cambridge online. Not so secretly, I'd been a fan of their work, and inviting cool researchers who you admire is one of the perks of organising a seminar series so you can subject your labmates to hear more about your own research interests!

Catherine had worked on one of the precursor papers in this tiny subliterature of "L2LMs" called BGPT and we found we had shared interests in expanding this to more languages. I'd also met Tatsuya Aoyama at NeurIPS and I really enjoyed chatting more about his work. I became increasingly convinced that research infrastructure should be something other people could actually use. There is a particular satisfaction in releasing something that another researcher can take, modify and build upon without having to ask your permission or reproduce years of work first. The open-source community has a wonderful culture of people making their work available, sharing infrastructure and helping each other solve problems. I wanted Beetle to squarely fall into this tradition.

The project also became a much more explicitly psycholinguistic endeavour through working with James Michaelov. I really enjoyed the opportunity to connect questions about language-model training with questions about how humans actually process language. And this was one of the many rude awakenings I had to deal with in this project since I hadn't necessarily seriously engaged with the amount of psycholinguistic literature. When I applied to Cambridge, I completed a Trinity College Essay competition on bilingualism and actually multilingual acquisition and processing was one of the things that drew me to think about the wonders of human language, which is certainly a uniquely human capability compared to animal communication for instance. Sadly, due to some more negative supervision experiences in my first year of Cambridge, I drifted away from studying psycholinguistics in my later years of my undergraduate studies. But Cambridge is wonderful in many ways, and I have to thank my college daughter, Laura Barbenel, in particular, for encouraging me to stretch myself and rediscover my initial interests in psycholinguistics!

Building something that didn't quite exist yet

At some point, the project became as much about building infrastructure as answering the original research question. I wanted to be able to construct models with different bilingual exposure histories, compare them against matched monolingual counterparts, and make the resulting checkpoints available for further research. This gradually turned into Beetle, a framework for controlled bilingual and multilingual pretraining, alongside its monolingual counterpart, Goldfish.

Beetle and Goldfish: perhaps not the most conventional pairing, but a rather good one for matched bilingual and monolingual models. Beetle is a nod to The Beatles and their distinctive drop-capital branding; Goldfish seemed like an appropriate companion for the monolingual models.

One of the key desiderata of our framework was dense intermediate checkpoints rather than just releasing the final models. It may be one of the most valuable aspects of the entire release. I spent a considerable amount of time developing the training framework, data pipelines, curricula, checkpointing and analysis infrastructure. What started as an experiment became something that could support a much larger family of experiments. I won't pretend that getting all of this working was always particularly exciting.

It wasn't always a ticket to ride. There were plenty of moments when the project felt more like a hard day's night. I spent far too much time checking configurations, inspecting training runs and trying to work out why something that should have worked didn't. I found issues with training runs; retraining models was not fun at all... But these experiences also changed how I think about experimental science. When you're building something at this scale, the engineering isn't separate from the research. The decisions you make about infrastructure determine what you can reliably investigate.

Ultimately, I'd much rather see Beetle become something that other people use, extend and occasionally disagree with than have it remain a self-contained project associated with one paper. A paper describes what you did and what you found. A framework gives someone else the opportunity to do something you haven't thought of yet. For Beetle, I'm especially excited about the possibilities for researchers interested in interpretability, developmental modelling and language acquisition. Having access to intermediate checkpoints means that people can investigate how representations change throughout training, how knowledge transfers between languages and whether different learning histories produce different internal structures.

A case for slow science and interdisciplinarity

I think one of the more difficult aspects of working on Beetle was learning to be patient with a project that simply refused to move at the pace I sometimes wanted it to.

Academic research, particularly in NLP and AI, is extraordinarily fast-moving; it's naturally stressful and high-pressure, and can be, in my opinion, increasingly negative when AI-generated reviews and peer pressure get thrown into the mix. With the rolling ARR cycles, there is always another submission deadline, another batch of papers appearing on arXiv, and another opportunity to get work out into the world. It's very easy to look around and feel as though everyone else is publishing considerably more than you are. When you're watching other researchers submit papers every few months, spending months building infrastructure, debugging experiments and trying to make a complicated research question experimentally tractable did make me feel that I was in some way "falling behind". I certainly felt that pressure. There were periods when I wondered whether I was making a mistake by investing so much time in something that wouldn't produce a publication for quite some time. There is a particular anxiety in watching the publication cycle move on while you're still trying to get your own experiments into a state where you trust them. You can spend months working on something and still have relatively little to show for it in the currency that academia most visibly rewards: accepted papers.

So having the paper selected for an Oral presentation at EMNLP 2026 was already incredibly rewarding. The paper was selected from a highly competitive ARR pool of approximately 17,000 submissions, placing it among the top 3%. I'm particularly pleased that a project built around a relatively niche question about bilingual language learning found an audience in the wider NLP community.

The recognition meant a lot to me, not simply because of the result, but because of what it represented after such a long journey. So much of the work was invisible: building the infrastructure, coordinating experiments, learning unfamiliar areas of psycholinguistics, working through engineering failures, revisiting analyses and having conversations that gradually changed how I thought about the original question.

The real test of Beetle will be what other people do with the models and how people choose to build on the framework. But it was a lovely reminder that there is value in taking the time to do something properly, even when the surrounding incentives sometimes make that difficult.

The experience has made me reflect on how I choose to engage in the publish-or-perish culture that is so dominant in AI at the moment. Perhaps the biggest thing that I've taken away from this experience is that research doesn't always have to move at the speed of the publication cycle. Some projects need time to become the projects they are supposed to become. And sometimes, it's worth resisting the urge to turn every intermediate result into another paper. I'm very glad I was able to breathe life into this project for so many months.

"After all, you get by with a little help from your friends." One of my favourite parts of working on Beetle has been the opportunity to work with people who think about AI in very different ways, including cognitive scientists, interpretability researchers, bilingualism researchers and applied AI researchers in education. Working with my supervisor, Paula Buttery, has been particularly enjoyable. What started with fairly niche questions about corpus linguistics, bilingual exposure and language acquisition gradually opened up much bigger conversations, including questions about Sovereign AI and the role of language technologies in different linguistic communities.

I think this captures something I particularly value about interdisciplinary research. There have been several moments during this project when I've felt as though I've been intellectually picked up and planted into an entirely new field. You start with a question you think you understand, have a conversation with someone who approaches it from a completely different perspective, and suddenly find yourself thinking about a much broader set of problems.

Interdisciplinary research is also really challenging to pitch. It took me many iterations of trial-and-error through discussions in Cambridge Language Sciences and with collaborators and visitors in local colloquiums and presentations in my group to figure out how to "sell" my work the best way.

That's really the essence of Beetle for me: the project itself took on its own life as it encountered new ideas, people and communities. I think I've come away understanding not just more about bilingual language learning, but also considerably more about the wider ecosystem in which language models are developed, evaluated and deployed.

Wrapping up a paper (or a PhD thesis) was described to me by a lecturer as a "divorce"; life moves on and the paper and the hard work moves on.

I don't think this is the end. And if you'll forgive one last Beatles reference: if this sounds like the end of a rather long and winding road, it really isn't. There are plenty of open questions and hopefully many more people who will take Beetle somewhere we haven't thought of yet.
