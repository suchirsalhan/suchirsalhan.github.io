Research at a glance

How do intelligent systems learn?

I study learning as it unfolds through training. Rather than only measuring what a finished model can do, I use small, human-scale language models as controlled experiments — tractable enough to inspect directly, yet rich enough to test hypotheses drawn from human language acquisition and cognition. Four connected questions run through the work.

Developmental interpretability

How do capabilities and internal representations emerge during training — and can we monitor that development before standard evaluations detect it? This connects interpretability to AI safety.

Learning dynamics & interpretability

Sample-efficient, human-scale learning

Humans learn language from far less data than today's models. Which architectures and training strategies let a model acquire real capabilities from child-sized data budgets? This drives the BabyLM and BabyVLM challenges.

Human-scale modelling

Multilingual & bilingual learning

Most people use more than one language, yet models of language processing are largely monolingual. By controlling what languages a model sees, and when, I test how linguistic experience shapes what it learns and generalises.

Beetle model suite

Tokenization & adaptive computation

Tokenization is usually a fixed preprocessing step. I treat it as a learned resource-allocation policy: can a model decide how much computation to spend on different parts of its input?

Selected projects

Open research infrastructure

Two open-source projects anchor my research programme — one a suite of controlled models, the other the framework that trains and studies them.

Beetle · Bilingual model suite

Beetle

The problem. Computational models of human language processing have focused heavily on monolingual speakers, even though most people in the world use more than one language.

What I build. Beetle is a framework and a collection of 330 small language models trained under controlled bilingual and monolingual settings — matched populations that differ in a specific aspect of their learning history.

Why it matters. This experimental control lets us test hypotheses about bilingualism — code-switching, language attrition, heritage learning — that are difficult to study in larger, less transparent systems.

PicoLM · Learning-dynamics framework

PicoLM

The problem. Studying how capabilities emerge requires training many small models and inspecting them checkpoint by checkpoint — infrastructure most researchers rebuild from scratch.

What I build. PicoLM is an open framework for pretraining, interpreting and tokenizing small language models, designed for hypothesis-driven experiments on learning dynamics.

Why it matters. It makes small-model research reproducible and comparable, giving a common foundation for the BabyLM community and my own work.

About

Background

I am a PhD candidate in Computer Science at the University of Cambridge (Gonville & Caius College), supervised by Professor Paula Buttery. My research sits at the intersection of machine learning, computational linguistics, cognitive science and AI safety.

I previously completed a BA and MEng in Computer Science and Linguistics at Cambridge, earning a Starred First (Class I with Distinction) and a Distinction. My master's work drew me into the BabyLM Challenge, and from there into the broader question of how models acquire their capabilities.

I co-organise the BabyLM and BabyVLM research challenges, lecture and supervise in Cambridge, and organise the weekly Cambridge NLIP seminars, open to the public.
