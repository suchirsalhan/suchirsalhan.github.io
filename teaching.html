More about these courses

Li18 Computational Linguistics

Li18 introduces statistical and neural approaches to language, from n-gram models and PCFG parsing through to modern transformer language models. My 2025–26 lectures cover language models and parsing (Lecture 7, computational syntax) and deep neural networks.

L95 Introduction to Natural Language Syntax and Parsing

L95 covers parsing algorithms (CKY, shift-reduce), categorial grammars, span-based constituency parsing, evaluation, lexicalised PCFGs and unification grammars, and recent topics such as discontinuous parsing. My handout covers language model evaluation with BLiMP and other minimal-pair benchmarks; I have also written notes and slides on tokenization.

Research supervision

ForCambridge MPhil and Part III students choosing a research project, and anyone asking about co-supervision.

I am looking for MPhil students who want to develop and understand language models. Projects sit in the Cambridge Natural Language and Information Processing (NLIP) group, with chances to work with researchers across computer science, linguistics and cognitive science. Depending on the project, a co-supervisor with expertise in language model fundamentals, AI safety, interpretability or computational psycholinguistics may also be possible. The proposed supervisors are me and Prof Paula Buttery.

I like projects that pair careful machine learning with a clear scientific question. The four areas below are starting points with example directions, not fixed specifications; we can shape a project around your interests, your background and the time an MPhil allows. The 2026–27 project proposals (PDF) have the full write-ups. My research is described on the research page.

1. Dynamic tokenization: learning what a model should process together

Architectures · Tokenization · Adaptive computation · Multilingual NLP

Tokenization is usually treated as preprocessing. Another view is that it is a resource allocation policy: it decides how much computation and memory a model spends on each part of its input. A familiar word, an unfamiliar name, a piece of code and a morphologically complex word may each benefit from a different granularity. Could a model learn to allocate computation dynamically instead of relying on a fixed vocabulary?

More about this project area

Recent work on byte-level and patch-based language modelling, and on transferring pretrained tokenizers into byte-level models, makes this question newly tractable.

Possible directions

  • Transferring pretrained tokenization. Can useful segmentation structure from a pretrained BPE model be transferred into a byte-level or tokenizer-free architecture without inheriting its fixed vocabulary?
  • Adaptive tokenization. Can a model learn when to process text as bytes, characters, subwords or longer patches, using signals such as uncertainty or information content?
  • Multilingual resource allocation. Does dynamic tokenization allocate computation better across languages with different writing systems, morphology and amounts of training data?
  • Interpreting segmentation decisions. Which features of an input make a model change its processing granularity, and do those decisions help downstream?

One direction I find especially interesting is whether tokenization can be transferred as a learned computational strategy rather than a fixed set of boundaries.

What you could do

Implement or adapt a tokenization architecture, design controlled comparisons against BPE and byte-level baselines, and evaluate on language modelling, multilingual tasks or efficiency. There may be room to analyse learned representations and segmentation behaviour too. A good fit if you enjoy deep learning systems, model architectures, efficient training, multilingual NLP, or the interface between language and computation.

Selected references

  • Cognetta et al. (2026), Tokenization: A Survey for Modern NLP.
  • Pagnoni et al. (2025), Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens.
  • Meister et al. (2026), LangMAP: A Language-Adaptive Approach to Tokenization.

2. Sample-efficient AI: models that learn more from less

Language model training · Efficient architectures · Cognitive science · Multimodal learning

Modern AI systems are trained on enormous amounts of data, yet people acquire language and other skills from comparatively little experience. I co-organise the BabyLM and BabyVLM challenges, which ask researchers to train models under constrained data budgets. The aim is to understand which architectural and learning choices let a model acquire useful capabilities from limited experience, not just to make a smaller model.

More about this project area

Cognitive science offers hypotheses about structured learning, curricula, memory and inductive biases; machine learning provides the tools to test and extend them.

Possible directions

  • Architectures for limited data. Do recurrence, parameter sharing, adaptive computation or other architectural choices help when training data are scarce?
  • Cognitively inspired learning. Do structured curricula, linguistic inductive biases or models of human language acquisition improve sample efficiency?
  • From text to multimodal learning. Do techniques that help language models learn from limited text also help models trained on restricted visual or speech data?
  • Evaluating what small models know. Develop or apply evaluations that separate genuine acquisition of a capability from memorisation, superficial task performance or dataset artefacts.

A strong project might start from a specific hypothesis about learning, run a controlled comparison, and establish when and why an approach works, not only whether it does.

What you could do

Train small models under controlled data budgets, develop new architectures or training strategies, and compare learning curves across model sizes, datasets or training regimes. Existing open-source frameworks and shared-task infrastructure give you a starting point. A good fit if you enjoy machine learning, neural network architectures, learning theory, cognitive science, or building AI systems under practical resource constraints.

3. Developmental interpretability: monitoring capabilities as they emerge

Mechanistic interpretability · AI safety · Training dynamics · Capability evaluation

Most evaluations look at a model after training, and most interpretability studies examine a model that is already trained. Both can miss what happens inside a model as it acquires a new capability, and whether we can detect that reliably. These questions matter for AI safety: an evaluation that only works at the end of training may give little warning about how a system is developing.

More about this project area

Capabilities need not emerge all at once. Training may build representations that later support particular behaviours, or create bottlenecks that limit what post-training can achieve. Rather than assuming an internal component is meaningful, we can test whether it predicts behaviour, whether interventions show a causal link, and whether findings replicate across checkpoints, seeds or model families. An internal signal that looks promising but does not generalise can be as misleading as a late evaluation.

Possible directions

  • Monitoring capability emergence. Can internal representations or activation patterns show a capability forming before behavioural evaluations reliably detect it?
  • Replicating interpretability findings. Do proposed circuits, neurons or linear representations persist across seeds, checkpoints and architectures?
  • Pretraining and post-training. Which capabilities are constrained by representations learned in pretraining, and which can later optimisation develop?
  • Reliable evaluation and AI safety. When do interpretability metrics give useful evidence about behaviour, and when do they give false reassurance?
  • Cultural and social capabilities. How do models acquire culturally specific knowledge, and which training conditions or representations go with reliable performance across cultural contexts?

What you could do

Analyse model checkpoints, design behavioural and internal evaluations, run activation interventions, or replicate published findings under controlled conditions. Projects range from focused empirical studies to broader investigations of how capabilities develop. A good fit if you enjoy mechanistic interpretability, AI safety, experimental machine learning, causal inference, or the scientific study of neural networks.

Related research

  • Chen et al. (2026), The Coverage Principle: How Pre-Training Enables Post-Training.
  • Zou, Salhan & Buttery (2026), Development of Linear Truth Encodings in Language Models: A Replication Study.
  • Polyakova-Reed et al. (2026), Claude Reinvestigates Herself: A Replication of Different Types of Syntactic Agreement Recruit the Same Units in LLMs.

The last two are part of our work on reproducibility and reliability in interpretability research.

4. Beetle: computational models of bilingual and multilingual language processing

Computational psycholinguistics · Multilingual models · Language acquisition · Cognitive modelling

Most people use more than one language, but computational models of language processing have mostly modelled monolingual speakers. I developed Beetle, a framework and suite of controlled bilingual language models for studying second-language processing. Because we control which languages a model sees, when, and how much, we can test hypotheses about how language history shapes behaviour.

More about this project area

The key advantage is experimental control. Instead of comparing unrelated models trained on different data, we can build matched populations that differ in one aspect of their learning history and test the consequences.

Possible directions

  • Code-switching. How do the balance and order of language exposure affect switching between languages within and across sentences?
  • Language attrition. When a model gets far more exposure to a second language, when does its first language deteriorate?
  • Heritage bilingualism. Can controlled training capture learning a minority language at home while a majority language becomes dominant?
  • Human–model alignment. Do model predictions and representations line up with human reading times, behavioural experiments or neural measurements?
  • Developmental trajectories. Do two models with similar final performance develop different representations because they met their languages in a different order or proportion?
  • Language production. How does prior language experience affect syntactic choices, lexical selection and cross-linguistic influence in generated text?

What you could do

Train or analyse controlled bilingual models, design computational psycholinguistic experiments, compare model predictions with human data, or study how multilingual training shapes representations and production. A good fit if you enjoy language, linguistics, cognitive science, multilingual NLP, experimental psychology, or testing theories of human learning with computational models.

Selected publications

  • Salhan, Arnett, Michaelov & Buttery (2026), Beetle: A Bilingual Model Suite for Modelling Second-Language Processing.
  • Kubiak, Salhan, Buttery & Moscoso del Prado Martín (2026), Distinct Patterns of Brain Alignment of Language Models across Linguistic Domains.
  • Barbenel et al. (2026), L1 Influence in L2 Language Models: A Human-Centric Approach.

What I look for

You do not need to have worked on all of these topics. I am interested in students who can frame a clear research question, work independently on technical problems, and look critically at their own results. Useful preparation depends on the project:

  • Machine learning: Python and PyTorch, neural networks, model training or empirical ML.
  • Computer systems: efficient training, computational constraints, architectures or experimental infrastructure.
  • Linguistics and cognitive science: formal linguistics, language acquisition, psycholinguistics or experimental methods.
  • Interpretability and evaluation: causal reasoning, statistics, reproducibility, or the limits of model evaluations.

These are complementary backgrounds, not a checklist. A student with a strong ML background might build a new tokenization architecture; a linguist might design controlled experiments on how bilingual experience changes model behaviour. I especially value projects that explain why an approach works, where it fails and what that tells us about learning, rather than reporting a benchmark gain.
