Beetle: A new way to experimentally study how language models learn.

What if we could study not just what language models know, but how their language experience shapes what they learn?

I created Beetle, an open-source experimental framework that lets researchers systematically manipulate the conditions under which language models develop: which languages they encounter, how much data they see, which tokenizer they use, and in what order they learn different languages. The result is a collection of 330 open models, spanning 13 first languages, five exposure curricula and three training scales, with dense checkpoints that make representational change measurable throughout training. This provides researchers with a new testbed for investigating how linguistic representations emerge, converge and diverge under different learning experiences — turning questions about language acquisition and multilingual model development into controlled, reproducible experiments.

The question

Beetle makes language-model development experimentally observable and controllable.

Most of what we know about language models comes from testing finished ones. We evaluate a trained model, compare its scores with another model's, and attribute the gap to whatever separates them, which is usually size, data and architecture all at once. That tells us what a model can do. It says little about how the ability formed, or which part of the model's experience produced it.

Beetle makes the learning experience the experimental variable. Models in a comparison share an architecture, training code and budget, and differ in one controlled respect, such as the point at which a second language enters the data. Every model is checkpointed densely, so a difference at the end of training can be traced back through the run to where it first appeared.

Experimental controls

A Beetle experiment is specified by six settings, each of which can be changed while the others stay fixed. They are set in a declarative YAML configuration rather than in bespoke training scripts, which is what makes large, systematic ablations practical.

  1. 01

    Language exposure

    Which languages a model encounters, and which it learns first.

    13 first languages; bilingual and monolingual runs
  2. 02

    Exposure curriculum

    When the second language arrives, and in what proportion.

    five curricula, B1–B5, drawn below
  3. 03

    Tokenisation

    How text is divided into the units the model learns from.

    set independently; tokenisers released
  4. 04

    Training budget

    How much data the model sees.

    100M, 2B or 24B tokens
  5. 05

    Corpus composition

    What the training text consists of.

    decontaminated, pretokenised data at each scale
  6. 06

    Developmental trajectory

    Where along training the model can be measured.

    log2 checkpoints with gradients and activations

Five exposure curricula

The curriculum is the variable most specific to Beetle. Holding the languages, model and budget fixed, it changes only when the second language appears and how much of the data it makes up.

The release

330 models285 bilingual45 monolingual13 first languagescurricula B1–B5100M / 2B / 24B tokensPicoDecoder backbone

The first release contains 330 models: 285 bilingual and 45 monolingual. Their 13 first languages were chosen to vary in typological distance from English and include German, Dutch, Chinese, Italian, Hindi, Russian, Turkish and Basque. The models span five exposure curricula and three training scales of 100M, 2B and 24B tokens.

The default backbone is a 125M-parameter, LLaMA-style PicoDecoder from PicoLM; encoder–decoder, mixture-of-experts and state-space variants are also supported. Checkpoints follow a log2 schedule and keep gradients and activations, so the suite can be used for interpretability work without retraining anything. For several of these languages, these are the first densely checkpointed models to be released.

Everything needed to reproduce the work is public: the checkpoints, the tokenisers, the decontaminated and pretokenised training data at all three scales, and the analysis pipelines. The software itself is three pip-installable libraries, covering controlled pretraining, exposure curricula, and analysis and interpretability.

What it can study

Because the models form matched populations, a question about learning history becomes a comparison between runs. Some of the questions the suite is built for:

  • When does a second language start to be represented, and does it arrive gradually or in jumps?dense checkpoints · curriculum
  • Does the order in which languages are learned change how much of the first language a model keeps?curriculum · language exposure
  • Does a curriculum work the same way for a first language close to English as for a distant one?language exposure · curriculum
  • How do representations of different languages converge or diverge as training goes on?language exposure · developmental trajectory
  • How much does segmentation shape cross-lingual representations, separately from the data?tokenisation
  • Can small models trained on modest data account for how people read in a second language?training budget · curriculum

The models are evaluated on MECO L2 reading times, BLiSS, CEFR, JFLEG and Mono/Multi-BLiMP, so behavioural and representational measures can be compared on the same runs.

First results

The EMNLP 2026 paper uses Beetle to ask how exposure structure affects second-language learning in small models. Three results stand out.

Small models match much larger ones on human reading times

Models trained with staged exposure fit human second-language reading times (MECO L2) as well as multilingual LLMs of up to 8B parameters, and often better, at a small fraction of their size.

Processing and grammaticality favour different curricula

Which curriculum is best depends on what you measure. Staged exposure gives the closest fit to online reading behaviour, while balanced exposure gives stronger offline grammatical judgements. Psycholinguists draw the same distinction between processing and competence in human second-language learners.

Learning happens in stages

With dense checkpoints the run itself can be inspected. Acquisition proceeds through phase transitions rather than smoothly, curricula interact with how close a language is to English, and the amount of the first language a model loses depends on both the curriculum and the second language. None of this is visible from the final checkpoint alone.

Language models are not simply trained. They develop under particular experiences. Beetle lets us control those experiences and study what they produce.
