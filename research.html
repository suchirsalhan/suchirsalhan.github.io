01

Research

How do intelligent systems learn?

I study how language models acquire knowledge, develop representations and learn to interact with the world.

My research brings together artificial intelligence, linguistics and cognitive science. I use small language models as experimental instruments: because they are relatively inexpensive and easier to inspect, I can study not just what they learn, but how learning happens.

Four questions guide my work.

What happens between seeing data for the first time and acquiring a new capability?

02 · Language

How does language shape learning?

Languages do not all give a learner the same problem.

  • Learning a second language

    How do the timing and amount of exposure to a second language shape what a model acquires?

    Papers: Beetle · L1 Influence in L2 Language Models · BLiSS

  • Learning beyond English

    What changes when training data and evaluation reach many languages, including those with little data?

    Papers: BabyBabelLM · Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER · Measuring Grammatical Diversity

  • How text is split

    Does the way text is segmented limit what a model can learn, and can segmentation be learned rather than fixed?

    Papers: ByteSpan · LangMAP · Tokenizer Cognitive Plausibility

03 · Representations

What representations emerge?

Models trained separately sometimes arrive at compatible representations, and I want to know when and why.

  • Convergence without joint training

    Do models trained on different languages reach shared representations, and does that make them mergeable?

    Papers: Cross-Lingual Alignment · Merged Goldfish Models · Universal Conceptual Representations

  • Finding structure inside models

    Can we locate the features and units behind specific linguistic abilities?

    Papers: Linguistic Universals via Sparse Autoencoders · Claude Reinvestigates Herself · Linear Truth Encodings

  • Linguistic theory as a guide

    What can linguistic theory tell us about where to look inside a model, and what can models tell linguists in return?

    Papers: Theoretical Linguistics and Causal Abstraction · Linguistics in the Age of Language Models

  20. Pico: A Modular Framework for Hypothesis-Driven Small Language Model Research (opens in a new tab)

    EMNLP 2025 System Demonstrations · Published

    A modular framework that lets you change one thing in a small language model's architecture or training and directly watch what it does. Small-model research should be a science, not guesswork.

    • Learning
    Authors (6) of Pico

    Richard Diehl-Martinez, David Demitri Africa, Yuval Weiss, Suchir Salhan, Ryan Daniels, Paula Buttery

    Venue: EMNLP 2025 Systems Demonstration · Suzhou, China · Conference or journal paper

    More resources for Pico
    • Paper (ACL Anthology): Pico (opens in a new tab)
    • arXiv preprint of Pico (opens in a new tab)
    • DOI for Pico (opens in a new tab)
    • Project page (Pico) (opens in a new tab)
    • Research summary of Pico

    Five-minute summary

    Building small and medium language models is still more art than science: tight parameter budgets make every design choice matter, yet researchers lack systematic ways to test and refine ideas. We introduce Pico, a lightweight, modular framework of two libraries that give researchers a sandbox for making targeted changes to a model's architecture or training procedures and directly observing the effect on its behaviour. To support reproducible experiments, we release a suite of baseline models, pico-decoder, trained under standardised conditions and open-sourced for the community. Case studies show how Pico supports iterative small-LM design and analysis.

  21. ByteSpan: Information-Driven Subword Tokenisation (opens in a new tab)

    ICML 2025 Tokenization Workshop · Published

    ByteSpan builds a subword vocabulary by grouping bytes a language model finds predictable, giving vocabularies that align with morphology better than BPE for English. Maybe tokenisers should learn morphology, not just compress.

    • Language
    Authors (5) of ByteSpan

    Zebulon Goriely, Suchir Salhan, Pietro Lesci, Julius Cheng, Paula Buttery

    Venue: ICML 2025 Tokenization Workshop (TokShop) · Vancouver, Canada (August 2025) · Workshop paper

    More resources for ByteSpan
    • Paper (arXiv): ByteSpan (opens in a new tab)
    • OpenReview for ByteSpan (opens in a new tab)
    • Research summary of ByteSpan

    Five-minute summary

    Recent dynamic tokenisers work directly on bytes and pool their latent representations into patches, much like word-segmentation models that mark boundaries where an autoregressive model's prediction error spikes. We ask whether grouping predictable bytes, rather than pooling their representations, can yield a useful fixed subword vocabulary. ByteSpan is an information-driven subword tokeniser that uses an external byte-level LM during training to find contiguous predictable byte sequences and group them into subwords. It produces efficient vocabularies with higher morphological alignment scores than BPE for English, and shows similar compression and Rényi efficiency across 25 languages.

  23. Meta-Pretraining for Zero-Shot Cross-Lingual Named Entity Recognition in Low-Resource Philippine Languages (opens in a new tab)

    MRL Workshop, EMNLP 2025 · Published

    Adding meta-learning to the pretraining of small decoder models lets them transfer named-entity recognition zero-shot to Philippine languages unseen during training. Teach small models to adapt and they travel to new languages.

    • Language
    Authors (5) of Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER

    David Demitri Africa, Suchir Salhan, Yuval Weiss, Paula Buttery, Richard Diehl-Martinez

    Venue: 5th Workshop on Multilingual Representation Learning (MRL), EMNLP 2025 · Suzhou, China · Workshop paper

    More resources for Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER
    • Paper (ACL Anthology): Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER (opens in a new tab)
    • arXiv preprint of Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER (opens in a new tab)
    • DOI for Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER (opens in a new tab)
    • Poster for Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER

    Five-minute summary

    Named-entity recognition in low-resource languages usually means finetuning very large multilingual LMs, which is often infeasible under memory or latency limits. We ask whether small decoder LMs can be pretrained so that they adapt quickly and transfer zero-shot to languages unseen during pretraining. To do this we replace part of the autoregressive objective with first-order model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML), testing on Tagalog and Cebuano, which are typologically similar yet differ in their actor/non-actor voice systems. Across four model sizes (11M–570M), MAML lifts zero-shot micro-F1 by 2–6 points under head-only tuning and 1–3 points after full tuning, and cuts convergence time by up to 8%. Gains are largest for single-token person entities that co-occur with the Tagalog case particles si and ni.

  24. Teacher Demonstrations in a BabyLM’s Zone of Proximal Development for Contingent Multi-Turn Interaction (opens in a new tab)

    BabyLM Workshop, EMNLP 2025 · Published First author Outstanding Paper Award

    ContingentChat, a Teacher–Student framework, teaches a 100M-word BabyLM to hold more grammatical, cohesive back-and-forth conversation. Tiny models can learn to actually hold a conversation.

    • Learning
    Authors (8) of Teacher Demonstrations

    Suchir Salhan, Hongyi Gu, Donya Rooein, Diana Galvan-Sosa, Gabrielle Gaudeau, Andrew Caines, Zheng Yuan, Paula Buttery

    Venue: BabyLM Workshop, EMNLP 2025 · Suzhou, China · Workshop paper

    More resources for Teacher Demonstrations
    • Paper (ACL Anthology): Teacher Demonstrations (opens in a new tab)
    • arXiv preprint of Teacher Demonstrations (opens in a new tab)
    • DOI for Teacher Demonstrations (opens in a new tab)
    • Research summary of Teacher Demonstrations

    Five-minute summary

    Child–caregiver dialogue has a property called contingency: prompt, direct, meaningful exchanges between the speakers. We introduce ContingentChat, a Teacher–Student framework that benchmarks and improves multi-turn contingency in a BabyLM trained on 100M words. Using a new alignment dataset for post-training, the BabyLM produces responses that are more grammatical and cohesive. Adaptive Teacher decoding strategies give only limited further gains. The work shows targeted post-training helps dialogue quality, and frames contingency as a hard goal for BabyLMs.

  25. Looking to Learn: Token-wise Dynamic Gating for Low-Resource Vision-Language Modelling (opens in a new tab)

    BabyLM Workshop, EMNLP 2025 · Published Outstanding Paper Award

    A lightweight vision-language model with token-wise dynamic gating learns from very little data and leans on images for content words, language for function words. The model learned when to look, nobody told it to.

    • Learning
    Authors (4) of Looking to Learn

    Bianca-Mihaela Ganescu, Suchir Salhan, Andrew Caines, Paula Buttery

    Venue: BabyLM Workshop, EMNLP 2025 · Suzhou, China · Workshop paper

    More resources for Looking to Learn
    • Paper (ACL Anthology): Looking to Learn (opens in a new tab)
    • arXiv preprint of Looking to Learn (opens in a new tab)
    • DOI for Looking to Learn (opens in a new tab)
    • Research summary of Looking to Learn

    Five-minute summary

    Training vision-language models on cognitively plausible amounts of data means rethinking how models combine words and images. Within the Vision track of the BabyLM Challenge 2025, we propose a lightweight decoder architecture with token-wise dynamic gating for adaptive fusion of linguistic and visual cues, feature modulation and channel attention to stretch limited visual information, and auxiliary contrastive objectives for visual grounding. Across five benchmarks (BLiMP, BLiMP Supplement, EWoK, Winoground and VQA) it matches or beats multimodal baselines. The dynamic gate also discovers interpretable patterns without supervision, favouring visual cues for content words and linguistic cues for function words.

    Five-minute summary

    Transformer language models use a fixed context window that has grown in step with pretraining dataset size, yet many BabyLM submissions default to much shorter sequences. We ask a simple question: what sequence length should we use when training BabyLMs? Using 100M words of training data and fixed compute budgets, we compare 125M-parameter Mamba and OPT models. Longer is often better, but the best length depends on both task and architecture. Shorter sequences are enough for grammatical generalization, while longer contexts help morphological analogical reasoning.

  32. Less is More: Pre-Training Cross-Lingual Small-Scale Language Models with Cognitively-Plausible Curriculum Learning Strategies (opens in a new tab)

    CoNLL 2024 BabyLM Challenge · Published First author

    Curricula that follow real theories of child language acquisition, applied language by language, can beat non-curriculum baselines across four language families. Copy how children actually learn, not just how much.

    • Learning
    • Language
    Authors (4) of Less is More

    Suchir Salhan* (equal contribution), Richard Diehl-Martinez, Zebulon Goriely, Paula Buttery

    Venue: CoNLL 2024 BabyLM Challenge (Paper Track) · Miami, Florida, USA (Nov 2024) · Workshop paper

    More resources for Less is More
    • Paper (ACL Anthology): Less is More (opens in a new tab)
    • arXiv preprint of Less is More (opens in a new tab)
    • Research summary of Less is More

    Five-minute summary

    Curriculum learning is a popular way to make Small-Scale Language Models (SSLMs) more cognitively plausible in the BabyLM Challenge, but it has rarely improved much over non-curriculum models. We ask whether theories of linguistic acquisition can specify finer-grained curricula. We build age-ordered corpora of Child-Directed Speech for four typologically distant language families and implement acquisition-inspired curricula cross-lingually. Comparing three curricula that replicate the predictions of acquisition theories (Growing, Inwards and MMM) on a standard SSLM architecture, we find fine-grained, language-specific curricula can outperform non-curriculum baselines.
