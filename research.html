Overview

I use small language models to study a large question: how does intelligence emerge through learning?

I work across artificial intelligence, cognitive science and linguistics. Cognitive science and linguistics supply the questions; small, human-scale language models are the instrument I use to answer them. Their constraints make learning visible: small enough to inspect checkpoint by checkpoint, yet rich enough to test hypotheses drawn from language acquisition. I study these models for their learning dynamics rather than their final scores: how representations form, how knowledge transfers, and how learned structure can be recombined.

Four questions organise the work:

  1. How do models learn? Sample efficiency, the phases of learning, and how post-training shapes behaviour.
  2. How does language shape learning? Multilingual, bilingual and second-language learning, tokenisation, and fair evaluation.
  3. What representations emerge? When independently trained models converge, and whether their representations line up with the brain.
  4. Can we trust small AI? Compact judges, small-model agents, and who a model is actually safe for.

Answers to these questions are useful beyond the lab. They help people build mergeable, efficient small models and agent pipelines that are cheaper to deploy than frontier systems, and they lay groundwork for multilingual AI that reaches beyond English into under-served languages.

The four questions my PhD tackles

Can we trace where representations come from?

When can independently trained models be merged without losing what each learned?

Can models learn their own words?

Can segmentation emerge during training, particularly for low-resource languages?

Can small models teach small models?

What determines a machine’s zone of proximal development, the range of tasks a learner can manage only with guidance?

What does scale obscure?

When small models reproduce human-like behaviour, what are they getting right that larger models get wrong?

Where the work applies

Model merging and small-model agents

For founders, industry teams and machine-learning researchers. Findings on representation convergence, mergeability and reinforcement-learning post-training carry over to practical systems: small models that can be composed and merged, and execution-aware agent pipelines built from efficient small language models. PicoLM (opens in a new tab) provides the modular tooling.

Multilingual and fairer AI

For people who use AI outside English and for the wider machine-learning community. I develop multilingual models, bilingual pretraining and evaluation that reaches under-served languages, including equitable benchmarks through the xBLiMPs grant. See Beetle and BabyBabelLM (opens in a new tab).

Education, culture and young people

For social scientists, educators and policymakers. I work on AI that supports learning: teacher-style demonstrations for language learning (Teacher Demonstrations), culturally grounded evaluation, child safety, and AI designed with and for young people. This connects to my public engagement through Per Capita Media (opens in a new tab).

Research themes

Each theme links to representative papers in the full catalogue below.

How do models learn?

Not just what a model knows at the end, but how it gets there.

Sample-efficient, human-scale language modelling. What can a model learn from the amount of language a child hears (10 to 100 million words), and what does that tell us about learning itself? Papers: Teacher Demonstrations, Best Sequence Length for BabyLM, Less is More, BabyLM Turns 4.

Learning dynamics and developmental interpretability. How do linguistic abstractions emerge over training? I study the phases of learning and open up small models to watch competence being assembled. Papers: Pico, Theoretical Linguistics and Causal Abstraction, Claude reinvestigates herself, Maximising Minimal Means.

Reinforcement-learning post-training and alignment. What limits reinforcement learning after pretraining, and how should models be aligned to human goals? Papers: Pedagogical Alignment of LLMs, Protocol Competence of Small LMs.

More about these themes

I build and study small language models trained on developmentally plausible data, in and around the BabyLM Challenge (opens in a new tab), a shared task on pretraining with a child-sized data budget. Under tight budgets each design choice (curriculum, sequence length, supervision signal) becomes measurable, so these models double as instruments for studying the mechanisms of acquisition.

I trace how abstractions emerge across training and look inside small models as they develop, drawing on linguistics and cognitive science. I help maintain open tooling, PicoLM (opens in a new tab), so these developmental-interpretability experiments are reproducible for the wider community.

On post-training, I study what constrains exploration under algorithms such as Group Relative Policy Optimisation (GRPO), and alignment objectives inspired by how teachers scaffold learners. Ongoing work includes From Entropy to Exploration: What Limits Post-Training under GRPO? and further work on pedagogical alignment.

How does language shape learning?

Languages do not all give a learner the same problem.

Multilingual, cross-lingual and bilingual modelling. Most language technology is built for English. I work on models that learn many languages, and on bilingual and second-language (L2) acquisition as a controlled setting for studying transfer. Papers: Beetle, BabyBabelLM, BLiSS, L1 Influence in L2 Models, Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER, Multimodal Grounding across Languages.

Tokenisation and input representation. How does the way text is segmented constrain what a network can learn, and can segmentation be learned rather than fixed? Papers: ByteSpan, LangMAP, Is Tokenizer Cognitive Plausibility just Fertility?

Equitable evaluation for low-resource languages. Benchmarks quietly encode which languages count. I build evaluation that is grammatically grounded and fair to languages with little data. Papers: BabyBabelLM, Measuring Grammatical Diversity, BLiSS, Phoneme Frequencies across Languages, Phonemes: Chance, Costs and Tiers, Diachronic Phoneme Frequencies, Convergent Phoneme Surprisal.

Cultural knowledge in language models. Whose culture does a model encode, and how stable is what it knows? Paper: Repertoires, Not Scores.

More about these themes

Beetle uses controlled bilingual pretraining to isolate how a second language is acquired and how cross-lingual transfer arises. It connects to multilingual benchmarks and curricula, such as BabyBabelLM (opens in a new tab), that reach beyond English into under-served languages.

I treat input representation as part of representation learning rather than preprocessing. I design information-driven ways of splitting text, grouping predictable bytes dynamically, and study language-adaptive tokenisation that lets segmentation shift with the language being modelled.

Through the xBLiMPs grant and related community work, I develop minimal-pair and grammaticality benchmarks that extend equitable linguistic evaluation to low-resource languages.

I also study cultural knowledge in pretrained models, including code-switching and cultural localisation, and argue that the instability of cultural responses is itself informative. Ongoing work includes Repertoires, Not Scores: Instability as Signal in Cultural Evaluation of LLMs.

What representations emerge?

When do independently trained models converge on compatible representations, and do those representations line up with the brain?

Representation convergence and model merging. I treat mergeability as a scientific probe of representation geometry, not only an engineering trick. Papers: Cross-Lingual Alignment Without Joint Training, Merged Monolingual Goldfish Models, Universal Conceptual Representations, Linguistic Universals via Sparse Autoencoders.

Brain alignment and mechanistic interpretability. Do model representations align with the brain, and can we locate the circuits behind specific linguistic competences? Papers: Theoretical Linguistics and Mechanistic Interpretability, Claude reinvestigates herself, Linear Truth Encodings, Linguistics in the Age of Language Models, Measuring Grammatical Diversity.

More about these themes

Ongoing work on convergence includes Mergeability is Constrained by Initialisation, Similarity is not Mergeability and Representations Bound the Generalizability of Model Merging.

I study how language-model representations align with the brain across linguistic domains, and use mechanistic interpretability to locate structure inside models, such as syntactic-agreement units and linear encodings of truth. Ongoing work includes Distinct Patterns of Brain Alignment of LMs across Linguistic Domains.

Can we trust small AI?

Capability is not the same as reliability.

Evaluation and trustworthy compact judges. Can small models be trusted to evaluate other models, and what evidence should we require before deploying them? Papers: Pedagogical Alignment of LLMs, Repertoires, Not Scores.

Small-model agents. Can small language models act as capable on-device agents? Paper: Protocol Competence of Small LMs.

AI safety and governance. Who is a model actually safe for? I study child audiences and the gap between recognising risk and acting on it. Papers: Repertoires, Not Scores, Pedagogical Alignment of LLMs, Linear Truth Encodings.

More about these themes

Evaluation is where scientific claims are won or lost. Ongoing work for NeurIPS 2026 workshops includes Can We Trust Small Language Models as Judges?, Towards Trustworthy Compact Judges and Governing the Evidence Standard for Trustworthy Compact AI.

On agents, I work on uses of small language models cheap enough to run on-device, including Execution-Aware Routing for On-Device SLM Agents (small language model agents) and the protocol competence needed for interactive language games.

On safety, ongoing work includes Models Recognise Child Audiences, But Child Safety Does Not Follow and related position papers. Ongoing work is listed on Google Scholar (opens in a new tab) as it appears.

    Topics: Learning dynamics & developmental interpretability · Sample-efficient learning

    Read the five-minute summary of Pico

    Building small and medium language models is still more art than science: tight parameter budgets make every design choice matter, yet researchers lack systematic ways to test and refine ideas. We introduce Pico, a lightweight, modular framework of two libraries that give researchers a sandbox for making targeted changes to a model's architecture or training procedures and directly observing the effect on its behaviour. To support reproducible experiments, we release a suite of baseline models, pico-decoder, trained under standardised conditions and open-sourced for the community. Case studies show how Pico supports iterative small-LM design and analysis.

  8. ByteSpan: Information-Driven Subword Tokenisation (opens in a new tab)

    Authors: Zebulon Goriely, Suchir Salhan, Pietro Lesci, Julius Cheng, Paula Buttery

    ICML 2025 Tokenization Workshop (TokShop) · Vancouver, Canada (August 2025) · Published · 2025

    ByteSpan builds a subword vocabulary by grouping bytes a language model finds predictable, giving vocabularies that align with morphology better than BPE for English. Maybe tokenisers should learn morphology, not just compress.

    Topics: Tokenisation & adaptive computation · Linguistics & cognitive science

    • Read the paper (arXiv): ByteSpan (ICML 2025 Tokenization Workshop) (opens in a new tab)
    • OpenReview for ByteSpan (opens in a new tab)
    • Read the research summary of ByteSpan
    Read the five-minute summary of ByteSpan

    Recent dynamic tokenisers work directly on bytes and pool their latent representations into patches, much like word-segmentation models that mark boundaries where an autoregressive model's prediction error spikes. We ask whether grouping predictable bytes, rather than pooling their representations, can yield a useful fixed subword vocabulary. ByteSpan is an information-driven subword tokeniser that uses an external byte-level LM during training to find contiguous predictable byte sequences and group them into subwords. It produces efficient vocabularies with higher morphological alignment scores than BPE for English, and shows similar compression and Rényi efficiency across 25 languages.

  10. Meta-Pretraining for Zero-Shot Cross-Lingual Named Entity Recognition in Low-Resource Philippine Languages (opens in a new tab)

    Authors: David Demitri Africa, Suchir Salhan, Yuval Weiss, Paula Buttery, Richard Diehl-Martinez

    5th Workshop on Multilingual Representation Learning (MRL), EMNLP 2025 · Suzhou, China · Published · 2025

    Adding meta-learning to the pretraining of small decoder models lets them transfer named-entity recognition zero-shot to Philippine languages unseen during training. Teach small models to adapt and they travel to new languages.

    Topics: Multilingual & bilingual AI · Sample-efficient learning

    • Read the paper (ACL Anthology): Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER (MRL Workshop, EMNLP 2025) (opens in a new tab)
    • arXiv preprint of Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER (opens in a new tab)
    • DOI for Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER (opens in a new tab)
    • View the poster for Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER
    Read the five-minute summary of Meta-Pretraining for Cross-Lingual NER

    Named-entity recognition in low-resource languages usually means finetuning very large multilingual LMs, which is often infeasible under memory or latency limits. We ask whether small decoder LMs can be pretrained so that they adapt quickly and transfer zero-shot to languages unseen during pretraining. To do this we replace part of the autoregressive objective with first-order model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML), testing on Tagalog and Cebuano, which are typologically similar yet differ in their actor/non-actor voice systems. Across four model sizes (11M–570M), MAML lifts zero-shot micro-F1 by 2–6 points under head-only tuning and 1–3 points after full tuning, and cuts convergence time by up to 8%. Gains are largest for single-token person entities that co-occur with the Tagalog case particles si and ni.

  11. Teacher Demonstrations in a BabyLM’s Zone of Proximal Development for Contingent Multi-Turn Interaction (opens in a new tab)

    Authors: Suchir Salhan, Hongyi Gu, Donya Rooein, Diana Galvan-Sosa, Gabrielle Gaudeau, Andrew Caines, Zheng Yuan, Paula Buttery

    BabyLM Workshop, EMNLP 2025 · Suzhou, China · Published · 2025 · First author · Outstanding Paper Award

    ContingentChat, a Teacher–Student framework, teaches a 100M-word BabyLM to hold more grammatical, cohesive back-and-forth conversation. Tiny models can learn to actually hold a conversation.

    Topics: Sample-efficient learning · Evaluation, safety & alignment · Linguistics & cognitive science

    • Read the paper (ACL Anthology): Teacher Demonstrations (BabyLM Workshop, EMNLP 2025) (opens in a new tab)
    • arXiv preprint of Teacher Demonstrations (opens in a new tab)
    • DOI for Teacher Demonstrations (opens in a new tab)
    • Read the research summary of Teacher Demonstrations
    Read the five-minute summary of Teacher Demonstrations

    Child–caregiver dialogue has a property called contingency: prompt, direct, meaningful exchanges between the speakers. We introduce ContingentChat, a Teacher–Student framework that benchmarks and improves multi-turn contingency in a BabyLM trained on 100M words. Using a new alignment dataset for post-training, the BabyLM produces responses that are more grammatical and cohesive. Adaptive Teacher decoding strategies give only limited further gains. The work shows targeted post-training helps dialogue quality, and frames contingency as a hard goal for BabyLMs.

    Read the five-minute summary of Best Sequence Length for BabyLM

    Transformer language models use a fixed context window that has grown in step with pretraining dataset size, yet many BabyLM submissions default to much shorter sequences. We ask a simple question: what sequence length should we use when training BabyLMs? Using 100M words of training data and fixed compute budgets, we compare 125M-parameter Mamba and OPT models.     Curriculum learning is a popular way to make Small-Scale Language Models (SSLMs) more cognitively plausible in the BabyLM Challenge, but it has rarely improved much over non-curriculum models. We ask whether theories of linguistic acquisition can specify finer-grained curricula.
